Research

What we study

Suicide, solitary death, mental health, and the unequal distribution of early death, seen through sociology, social welfare, and social policy.

Research areas

Suicide and suicidal thoughts

Why suicidal thoughts have risen among older Koreans, why adolescent girls report more of them than boys, how religion shapes acceptance of suicide, and how risk is distributed across Korean counties.

Solitary death (godoksa) and isolation

Who dies alone in Korea, and how individual circumstances combine with the places people live. We link this to social isolation and to how older adults actually spend their days.

Inequality in death

How violence, environmental hazards, and local disadvantage push the risk of early death onto particular communities, in Korea, Mexico, and the United States.

Mental health, welfare, and prevention policy

How welfare services, outreach, and local prevention programs reach (or miss) people at risk, and how research can become evidence practitioners and policymakers can use.

Time, aging, and mental health

Using Korean and American time-use diaries, we look at how leisure, work, care, and time alone relate to mental health across the life course, especially in later life.

Three lenses on one problem

Sociology asks why risk concentrates in some groups and places. Social welfare asks which services and outreach actually reach people before it is too late. Social policy asks which programs, in which communities, make a measurable difference. Most of our projects start in the first lens; more and more of them end in the second and third.

We also work with computer science colleagues at Korea University on applying AI methods to public data, including a BK21 collaboration proposal focused on Sejong, Korea's administrative capital.
