Published research

Violence, suicide, and family life

When violence rises in a community, what happens to the people who live there? Using Mexico as a case, we follow its effects on suicide and on whether couples marry, move in together, or stay together.

  • 2026"Does Exposure to Community Violence Predict Higher Suicide Rates? The Case of Mexico." Caudillo & Lee, Population and Development Review. doi:10.1111/padr.70080 · Featured in PNAS Journal Club
    Key finding: Local homicide spikes were followed by a short-lived rise in suicide, strongest in the same month and driven mainly by men (municipal death records, 2000–2019).
  • 2023"Community Violence and the Stability of Marriages and Cohabitations in Mexico." Caudillo & Lee, Social Forces. doi:10.1093/sf/soac140
    Key finding: For less educated women, a recent rise in local violence was associated with a 71% higher risk that their first union dissolved; no clear link for more educated women.
  • 2023"Local Violence and Transitions to Marriage and Cohabitation in Mexico." Caudillo & Lee, Journal of Marriage and Family. doi:10.1111/jomf.12890
    Key finding: For less educated women, a recent rise in local violence was associated with about 28% lower chances of entering marriage as a first union.

Youth suicide and mental health

Why do Korean adolescent girls report suicidal thoughts more often than boys? We look at the social world teenagers live in, especially the gender makeup of their peer groups.

  • 2023"Why Is the Korean Girls' Suicidal Ideation Rate Higher than the Boys' Rate? The Role of Gender Heterogeneity in Peer Groups." Bae & Lee, PLoS ONE. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0290072
    Key finding: Girls were about twice as likely as boys to report suicidal impulses (37.7% vs. 18.5%), and having different-gender friends was linked to higher risk, mainly for girls.

Trust, values, and social attitudes

How trust and values shape the choices people make about their health and how they see others: vaccination decisions in the U.S., and two decades of change in Korean attitudes toward homosexuality.

  • 2024"Changing Attitudes Toward Homosexuality in South Korea, 1996–2018." Lin & Lee*, Social Science Research. doi:10.1016/j.ssresearch.2023.102972
    Key finding: The share of Koreans saying homosexuality is never justifiable fell from 67% in the 1990s to 24% in the latest wave; among young adults, women were more accepting than men.
  • 2023"Compliance, Procrastination and Refusal: American COVID-19 Vaccination Trust and Value Orientation." Tzeng, Huang & Lee*, Vaccine. doi:10.1016/j.vaccine.2023.06.060
    Key finding: Low trust in vaccine effectiveness and seeing vaccination as a personal choice were the strongest predictors of delaying or refusing COVID-19 vaccination, more than politics or health risk.

Work, family, and health

How the conditions of work and family life show up in health: workplace stress and smoking among Korean workers, and disagreement between partners over pregnancy intentions in the U.S.

  • 2024"Racial-Ethnic Disparities in Dyadic Pregnancy Intentions Preceding Births in the United States." Caudillo, Kim, Lee & Liu, Population Research and Policy Review. doi:10.1007/s11113-024-09860-z
    Key finding: About a quarter of U.S. births (26%) were conceived when partners disagreed about the pregnancy, with large racial-ethnic differences.
  • 2021"Work Environment and Workers' Smoking: The Impact of Affect and Social Support Among Korean Workers." Gu, Lee & Lee, Journal of Asian Sociology. doi:10.21588/dns.2021.50.2.001
    Key finding: Less job autonomy and less flexible working hours were associated with smoking, partly through negative emotions; social support was associated with less smoking.

Three lenses on one problem

Sociology asks why risk concentrates in some groups and places. Social welfare asks which services and outreach actually reach people before it is too late. Social policy asks which programs, in which communities, make a measurable difference. Our published work so far sits mostly in the first lens; the next papers move into the second and third.

We also work with computer science colleagues at Korea University on applying AI methods to public data, including a BK21 collaboration proposal focused on Sejong, Korea's administrative capital.

Digital access, AI, and well-being

Using the Korea Digital Divide Survey (2019–2023), which covers older adults, people with disabilities, low-income households, farmers and fishers, North Korean defectors, and marriage migrants, we ask whether access to digital technology and AI improves life satisfaction and mental health, and for whom. With Prof. Cho. If it does, teaching people to use technology is itself a kind of health intervention.
