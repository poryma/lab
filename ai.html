AI Principles · Version 2, November 2026

How we use AI, and how we don't

AI is now part of how research gets done. We would rather talk about it openly than pretend it isn't there, or let everyone quietly make up their own rules.

Why we wrote this

When students started asking me whether they were "allowed" to use ChatGPT, I realized most of them had been given either a flat ban or no guidance at all. Neither helps. A ban drives use underground; silence leaves people guessing, and the anxious ones guess wrong.

Our lab studies suicide, solitary death, and inequality in who dies early. That work runs on sensitive data and has real consequences for prevention policy and for how the people we study are remembered. So our rules are a little stricter than they would be in some fields, and we try to explain the reasoning behind each one.

Many of our graduate students in Social Welfare will go on to work in welfare agencies, counseling centers, and local government, where AI tools are arriving fast and the people affected are often the most vulnerable. We want them to leave knowing how to use these tools well, and when not to.

Which tools, and why more than one

We don't standardize on a single model. Claude is the lab's main assistant for coding and writing support right now, Codex handles some programming work, and ChatGPT, the first tool many of us learned on, is one we want back in regular use. The reason is methodological, not brand loyalty: when two models give different answers about a model specification, a translation, or a summary of a paper, the disagreement is a flag. We go to the source.

Beyond research: teaching and everyday academic work

Research is only one place AI shows up in an academic life. I'm just as interested in teaching and in the ordinary work around it, and in helping the next generation of AI users build habits that make them more capable rather than more dependent. A few things we're building and testing:

Research

One verified record

When several AI sessions work on the same project, they can quietly contradict each other. We keep one source of truth for key facts and decisions, and every session reads from it and writes back to it, so the next session (or the next student) starts from what was actually checked.

Teaching

Reusable course pipelines

Small tools that turn the work I already do into course materials: syllabus builders that check every reading's DOI, illustration briefs for ChatGPT image generation, exam builders, bilingual glossaries, and step-by-step guides for posting materials to the course site.

Productivity

Across devices

The same workflows run on a Windows desktop, a Mac mini, a laptop, and a phone, with shared folders, scheduled tasks, and one-button shortcuts on a Stream Deck. The aim is less time on logistics and more on students and ideas.

Next generation

Teaching students to build their own

In class, students don't just use AI; they design small workflows, write their own AI principles, and keep an AI use log. The point is judgment: knowing what to hand over, what to keep, and how to check.

Six principles

PRINCIPLE 1

Augment, don't automate

AI should make you a better researcher, not a faster replacement for one. If a tool is doing the part of the work you're supposed to be learning (forming the question, choosing the model, interpreting the result), stop and do it yourself first. A pot made by hand and one made by a machine can look alike; the process is what changes you.

PRINCIPLE 2

You are the author

Whatever ends up in a paper, a thesis, or a slide deck with your name on it is yours, including every mistake. "The AI said so" is never an explanation. Your reasons have to be your own.

PRINCIPLE 3

Verify everything that matters

Every citation gets opened and read. Every number gets traced back to the data. Every line of code gets run and its output checked. When two models disagree, that's a flag to go back to the source. Language models are fluent, and fluency is easy to mistake for accuracy.

PRINCIPLE 4

Protect the people in the data

No identifiable, restricted, or licensed microdata goes into a chat interface: no death records, no linked survey files, no interview transcripts. Information that was shared in one context (a clinic, a survey, a welfare office) should not quietly flow into another. Describe your data's structure, use synthetic examples, or work with variable names only.

PRINCIPLE 5

Say what you used

Disclose substantive AI use to co-authors, to me, and to journals when their policies ask for it. Disclosure isn't a confession. It's part of a transparent method, just like reporting your software, and it keeps the trust between a writer and the person reading their work.

PRINCIPLE 6

Keep talking about it

We discuss what worked, what failed, and what made us uneasy at lab meetings. The norms here should come from experience, not from fear or hype, and they should be decided together.

Extra care for research on suicide and death

  • Follow safe-messaging guidance. AI-drafted text about suicide can slip into sensational language or describe methods. Every public-facing sentence on this topic gets human review against established reporting guidelines.
  • Study places and conditions, not individuals. We don't build models that score individual people's risk. Our unit is the community, the policy, the social condition.
  • Don't let a model categorize a death. Classifying causes, circumstances, or "types" of death is an interpretive act with consequences. AI can help organize a coding scheme; people apply it.
  • Ask who gets counted. Administrative data miss people: those without stable housing, without family, outside the welfare system. Models trained on these data inherit the gaps. Models trained mostly on Western, English-language text may also misread Korean family and social contexts.
  • Look after yourself. This material is heavy. Using AI to move faster through it isn't a reason to skip breaks or skip talking to someone.

For graduate students

Your thesis is where you learn to be a researcher, so the bar for your own thinking is higher there than anywhere else.

  • Keep a short AI log for each project: date, tool, what you asked, what you kept. Three lines per session is enough. It protects you if questions come up later, and it helps you see your own habits.
  • Before using AI on a new kind of task, do it once by hand. You can't evaluate output you couldn't have produced.
  • Check the rules that apply to you: Korea University's policies, your data provider's terms of use, your IRB approval, and the target journal's AI policy. When they conflict, the strictest one wins.
  • If you're unsure whether something is okay, ask before, not after. No one in this lab gets in trouble for asking.

During the preparation of this work, the author(s) used [tool, version] to [specific task, e.g., translate Stata code into R and suggest edits for clarity in Sections 2–3]. All analyses were run and checked by the author(s). All cited sources were read in full. No individual-level data were shared with the tool. In the classroom

In AI and Society (Fall 2026), students don't just read about AI; they test it. They give the same prompt to ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude and compare what comes back, audit image generators for gender and cultural bias, check what platforms have inferred about them, and rebuild a risk-scoring exercise with the COMPAS data. The goal is graduates who can use these tools well and say clearly when they shouldn't be used at all.

How we make our course illustrations. The illustrations on this page were made for that course. For each lecture I write a brief: the idea a section needs to carry, a fixed style and palette, and hard constraints (no text, no logos, no real people). ChatGPT generates the image from the brief, and each one is checked against the brief before it goes into a lecture. It's a small, visible example of the principle above: the machine draws, but the idea, the constraints, and the final judgment stay with us.
