About the lab

South Korea has one of the highest suicide rates in the developed world, and every year more people here die with no one beside them and are found days or weeks later. Behind those numbers are patterns. Age, gender, region, work, family, and neighborhood all shape who is at risk. Our lab tries to make those patterns visible, carefully and without losing sight of the people inside the data.

We work mostly with large population data: death records, national time-use diaries, and long-running surveys of Korean adults, adolescents, and older adults. Some projects reach beyond Korea, including work on how community violence in Mexico shapes suicide, marriage, and family life.

I trained in sociology and demography at the University of Maryland and taught at Gettysburg College and Arkansas State University before coming home to Korea University in 2025, where I did my undergraduate degree.

Research

Suicide and suicidal thoughts

Why suicidal thoughts have risen among older Koreans, why adolescent girls report more of them than boys, how religion shapes acceptance of suicide, and how risk is distributed across Korean counties.

Solitary death (godoksa) and isolation

Who dies alone in Korea, and how individual circumstances combine with the places people live. We link this to social isolation and the way older adults actually spend their days.

Inequality in death

Mortality is not shared evenly. We study how violence, environmental hazards, and local disadvantage push the risk of early death onto particular communities, in Korea, Mexico, and the United States.

Time, aging, and mental health

Using Korean and American time-use diaries, we look at how leisure, work, care, and time alone relate to mental health across the life course, especially in later life.

Active projects

Most of these are in analysis or under review. Each one asks a version of the same question: how do social conditions get under the skin and, in the worst cases, end lives early?

Suicide in an aging Korea

Why suicidal thoughts have risen among older Koreans, and how that rise differs by gender, region, and generation.

Adolescent suicidal ideation

How peer groups, family, and multicultural background shape trajectories of suicidal thoughts among Korean teenagers.

Solitary death in South Korea

Individual and contextual patterns behind godoksa, with colleagues at Portland State and the University of Maryland.

Childhood adversity and isolation

How adverse childhood experiences echo through adult social isolation, loneliness, and mental health.

Religion, meaning, and health

Religiosity and attitudes toward suicide, and religion's uneven role in health inequality, using Korean and cross-national surveys.

Polarization and suicide

Whether social and political division leaves measurable marks on population mental health and suicide.

Violence and self-harm in Mexico

Following up our Population and Development Review paper on how local violence raises suicide rates.

Time, leisure, and later life

National time-use diaries on how older adults spend their days alone or with others, and what that means for mental health.

Data we work with include national death records, the Korean Time Use Survey, the Korean General Social Survey, the World Values Survey, the Asian Barometer, the Global Flourishing Study, and Korean digital-divide surveys.

How AI fits into our work

ChatGPT was the first AI tool I used in research. When I started building analysis pipelines and small research tools through conversational coding, most of that moved to Claude, which is my main assistant today, with Codex alongside it. I'd like ChatGPT back in that mix. Giving the same task to more than one model turns out to be one of our best error checks: where they disagree is usually where we need to look harder.

We use AI to extend what we can do, not to do our thinking for us. In practice that means these tools show up in the unglamorous parts of research: translating analysis code between Stata, R, and Python, catching errors when we harmonize survey waves, reading across literatures in more than one language, and arguing with a draft before a reviewer does.

What AI doesn't do here: decide what a finding means, write the paper, invent a citation we haven't checked, or see data that could identify a person. Studying suicide and death raises the stakes. A careless summary or a hallucinated statistic isn't just an embarrassing mistake; it can misinform prevention policy and misrepresent people who can no longer speak for themselves.

We also think the people we train deserve straight answers about what's allowed. So we wrote them down.

Teaching

All of my courses at Korea University are taught in English. In Spring 2026, Sociology of Health and Medicine and Sociology of Disaster were rated 4.92 and 4.93 out of 5 by students.

In Fall 2026 I started AI and Society, a hands-on course on how technology and society shape each other, from movable type to large language models. Most weeks have a lab: students put the same prompt to ChatGPT, Gemini, and Claude and compare what comes back, run bias audits on image generators, check what platforms have inferred about them, and work through the COMPAS recidivism data. The point isn't to make students fans or critics of AI. It's to make them people who ask good questions about it.

Other courses: Korean Society, Sociology of Disaster, Health and Medicine, a seminar on quality of life, and workshops on quantitative and longitudinal methods.

Join the lab

I'm looking for undergraduate and graduate students who care about inequality and aren't afraid of data. You don't need to arrive knowing Stata or Python. You do need curiosity, patience with messy data, and respect for the subject. This research involves reading about people's deaths, and we look after each other while doing it.

If you're interested, email me a short note about what you'd like to study and why. Please read our AI guidelines first, since they apply to everyone working with us.

If you are struggling. In Korea you can call or text the suicide prevention line at 109, any time, day or night. Outside Korea, please contact your local crisis service. You don't have to be in immediate danger to reach out.
